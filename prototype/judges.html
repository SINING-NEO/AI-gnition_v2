AI-gnition · proposal briefing

Use the orders a business already has. Talk only when a product is due. Prove the conversation worked.

We propose a retention system that predicts the next purchase from existing order history, then lets a separate set of rules decide who may be contacted. The conversation asks for one repeat purchase. A holdout group is how the effect gets measured.

Hackathon pitch · Calculations · Customer page · First agent loop

How might we turn the customer data a business already holds into personalised, automated conversations that measurably increase retention and repeat purchase?

What we are proposing

A shop already stores who bought what, and when. We use that history for two predictions: how many days until the next purchase, and which product that purchase is likely to be. Those predictions do not send the message. Consent, stock, a contact window, and a 20% holdout do.

The conversation

One job per person. If they are due, ask them to repeat the product their history supports. If they are late, ask if they need help restocking it. The sentence may use only a product, a date, a price, and an offer that already exist in the records.

The proof

Eligible customers are split before any send. About 80% can receive the message. About 20% are held out. After 14 days, repeat purchases in the two groups are compared. If they match, the conversation did not cause the purchase.

What the judges can treat as demonstrated

8.19

days of timing error

Ridge regression on held-out fictional orders. The same customers’ own median gap was 10.19 days.

77.9%

next product correct

Softmax classifier, 12 products. Guessing “the same product again” was right 49.1% of the time.

250

drafts ready to review

From 3,000 synthetic customers. 209 were held out. 522 had no consent and were suppressed. None were emailed.

The demonstration retailer is fictional, called Pantry & Co. The workbook, the trained weights, the policy file, and the review queue are in the repository.

The process we followed

  1. 1

    Start from the question, not from a chatbot

    The required outcome is a measured repeat purchase. A general website chat does not use the orders the business already holds, and it does not create a comparison group.

  2. 2

    Test whether the data can support the idea

    On synthetic books, a “regular gap” rule still marked 39% of an irregular catalogue as if people were replenishing. Requiring a repeated product and a 14–45 day cycle kept 66.9% of a consumables mix and 0% of the irregular book.

  3. 3

    Make the model beat a simple rule

    A small network was useful when the last two products changed the gap. On a steady 28-day habit it was almost the same as the median. The later MVP therefore kept an interpretable ridge model and published both errors.

  4. 4

    Separate prediction from permission

    The model scores timing and product. Code checks consent, stock, cooldown, the due window, the holdout bucket, and a cap of 250. A critic rejects a product, date, or discount that is not in the brief.

  5. 5

    Build the path a customer would see

    The scoring run writes a review queue and a personal landing page: the in-stock reorder, an approved add-on if one exists, the current price, and links to FAQ and policies. The chat panel is a preview of the handover, not a live assistant.

  6. 6

    Next, with a sponsor

    Replace the fictional FAQ, policies, and catalogue. Connect MantaGO so the conversation is grounded in those sources and can hand over to a person. Then run one consented holdout and read repeat purchases at 14 days.

What we are not claiming

No measured sales lift yet

The holdout is assigned and logged. There are no later purchases to compare, because no campaign was sent. The 8.19 days and 77.9% describe prediction quality, not incremental revenue.

No live conversation layer yet

MantaGO is the planned place for the grounded reply and the human handover. The payload is prepared. It has not been transmitted, and the embedded chat is a demonstration.
